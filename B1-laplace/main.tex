\section{From Functions to Transforms}

\subsection{The Laplace Transform}

Let \(f(t)\) be a function defined for \(t \geq 0\).
Its Laplace transform is defined by

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}
    = F(s)
    = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-st} f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,
    \qquad s > 0.
\end{equation}

The transform changes a function of time into a function of the new
variable \(s\).\footnote{The variable \(s\) is commonly interpreted as a
complex-frequency variable.}

Symbolically,

\begin{equation}
    f(t)
    \xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}
    F(s).
\end{equation}

The inverse Laplace transform reverses this process:

\begin{equation}
    f(t)
    = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\}.
\end{equation}

The main purpose of the method is to replace differentiation and
integration in the time domain with simpler algebraic operations in the
transform domain~\cite{kreyszig2011}. A concise overview is available
from the
\href{https://mathworld.wolfram.com/LaplaceTransform.html}
{Laplace Transform entry on MathWorld}.

\subsection{A Simple Example}

For the constant function \(f(t)=1\),

\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}\{1\}
        &= \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t \notag \\
        &= \left[
            -\frac{1}{s}e^{-st}
           \right]_{0}^{\infty} \notag \\
        &= \frac{1}{s}.
\end{align}

Similarly,

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}\{t\}
    = \frac{1}{s^2},
    \qquad
    \mathcal{L}\{t^2\}
    = \frac{2}{s^3}.
\end{equation}

More generally,

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}\{t^n\}
    = \frac{n!}{s^{n+1}},
    \qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots
\end{equation}

\section{Application to an RC Circuit}
\subsection*{Circuit Model}
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.45\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{rc_circuit.png}
    \caption{A simple series RC circuit}
    \label{fig:rc}
\end{wrapfigure}
Figure~\ref{fig:rc} shows a simple resistor--capacitor
circuit connected to an input voltage source.
The resistor limits the current, while the capaci-
tor stores electrical energy.

For a resistor $R$ and capacitor $C$, Kirchhoff’s
voltage law gives
\begin{equation}
    RC \frac{dv_{C}(t)}{dt}  + v_{C}(t) = v_{in}(t)
    \label{eq:five}
\end{equation}
where $v_C (t)$ is the voltage across the capacitor.
Assuming $v_C (0) = 0$, taking the Laplace
transform of Equation~\ref{eq:five} gives
\begin{align}
    RC[sV_{C}(s) - v_{C}(0)] + V_{C}(s) &= V_{in}(s) \notag \\
    \implies RCsV_{C}(s) + V_{C}(s) &= V_{in}(s)\notag\\
    \implies V_{C}(s)(RCs + 1) &= V_{in}(s)
\end{align}
The transfer function is therefore
\begin{equation}
    H(s) = \frac{V_{C}(s)}{V_{in}(s)} = \frac{1}{RCs+1}
\end{equation}
For a constant input $V_{0}$,
\begin{equation}
    V_{in}(s) = \frac{V_{0}}{s}.
\end{equation}
Thus,
\begin{equation}
    V_{C}(s) = \frac{V_{0}}{s(RCs+1)}
\end{equation}
Taking the inverse transform gives
\begin{equation}
    v_{C}(s) = V_{0}(1-e^{ -t/(RC) })
    \label{eq:nine}
\end{equation}
    
The quantity $\tau = RC$ is called the time constant of the circuit. A larger value of $\tau$ causes the capacitor to charge more slowly \cite{ogata2010}.

\section{How Laplace Analysis Is Used}

The Laplace transform is useful in many areas:
\begin{description}
    \item[Differential equations] $\bigoplus$ initial conditions are included automatically \\
    $\bigoplus$ derivatives become algebraic expressions.
    \item[Electrical circuits] $\bigoplus$ voltages and currents are studied in the s-domain; \\
    $\bigoplus$ components lead to transfer functions.
    \item[Control and signal systems] $\bigoplus$ stability can be studied through poles; \\
    $\bigoplus$ common test inputs include:
    \begin{itemize}[label=$\triangleright$]
        \item he unit-step input;
        \item the impulse input;
        \item sinusoidal inputs.
    \end{itemize}
    
\end{description}

\blue{In the time domain, a system may be described by a differential equation,} whereas \red{in the Laplace
domain, the same system is often described by a rational algebraic expression.}
